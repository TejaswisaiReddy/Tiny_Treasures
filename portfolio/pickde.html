Why this project

Same food. Different price.

A startup in India wanted to take on the big delivery apps with one promise: you pay the shop’s price, plus a small delivery fee, and nothing else.

Before designing anything, I wanted to know if people really felt the problem — so I asked 78 of them. 87% said they would back an app like this.

“We need a transparent app without difference in price and quality.”

— a survey respondent
Context & constraints
The stakes

A promise the business depends on.

The startup’s whole pitch was the shop’s own prices. If people couldn’t check that claim themselves, there was no reason to switch apps.

My constraints

Volunteer, solo, no live data.

No budget, no engineers and no access to real restaurant pricing. I worked from public market figures and a survey I ran myself.

The tension

Honest costs more taps.

Every fee shown up front makes the app trustworthy — and makes the checkout screen longer to read.

02

The problem

22-question survey · 78 responses · Nov 2024 – Jan 2025

The menu says one price. The app charges another.

Restaurants pay apps 16–30% commission per order, and app prices run about 17% above the menu (Jefferies, 80 restaurants, 2022). The platform fee alone went from ₹2 to ₹17.58 between 2023 and 2026. So I asked people what that feels like.

03

The turning point

What a design review changed.

My first version said the price was fair. It didn’t prove it.

A design review showed the gaps. “Same as menu price” was only a label. Fees didn’t match from one screen to the next. Some text was too small and too faint to read. And I had promised an “instant refund” the business couldn’t guarantee.

Before the review
  • “Same as menu price” was only a claim
  • Fees and distances differed between screens
  • “Instant refund” promised
  • Faint borders, light stars, 9.5–11.5 px text
After
  • In-store price, check date and “Report a mismatch” beside it
  • One rule everywhere; full bill in cart, payment and confirmation
  • Eligibility and timing stated; status shown in Orders
  • Borders 3.4:1, stars 3.8:1, minimum text 12 px, clear focus states
What it taught me

A fair-price promise only works if people can check it. Trust has to be designed into every price and fee.

04 — Key decisions

Three decisions, and why.

Decision 01

Prove the price — don’t just promise it.

Why

People had heard “fair price” before.

The alternative

A trust badge, with the promise buried in the terms.

The trade-off

Extra price detail adds clutter.

What I chose

Menu price, check date and a report link.

Decision 02

One fee rule. The same bill, everywhere.

Why

Fees changed from screen to screen.

The alternative

Show the total only at checkout, like most apps.

The trade-off

Repeating the bill makes screens longer.

What I chose

One fee rule. The same bill everywhere.

Decision 03

Fix problems from the order, not a chat.

Why

76% had a wrong or missing item.

The alternative

A support chat with an agent.

The trade-off

Fixed choices cover fewer edge cases.

What I chose

Three taps: item, reason, refund or re-delivery.

05

Outcome & reflection

The outcome

16 screens where every price can be checked against the shop’s own menu, one fee rule runs through cart, payment and confirmation, and a missing item is reported from the order in three taps.

How I checked it

A structured design review against the brief and WCAG 2.2 AA, screen by screen. Contrast was measured (8.6:1 on action buttons); everything else is design judgement, not user evidence.

What I’d test next

A moderated test with 5–8 people from the survey group: can they check a price, read the bill and report a missing item without help?

A pattern I’m working on

I let a label do the work of proof. Now I ask of every claim in an interface: what would let someone check this for themselves?
