Why I built this

I’m a parent. I lived this problem first.

Kids outgrow clothes, shoes and toys in months. As a parent, I knew how much time it takes to sell those things online — and how hard it is to find the right size, in good condition, from someone you can trust.

Being a parent helped me understand the frustration. Talking to 25 other parents helped me look beyond my own experience — and design for theirs.

“It takes time to sell them and buy them, even online.”

— a parent, from my survey
Context & constraints
The stakes

A thesis, judged on evidence.

This was my master’s thesis, so every design decision had to trace back to research — and still feel like a product a parent would actually open.

My constraints

Solo, six months, no dev team.

One designer, no budget and no engineering support. Payments, photo analysis and verification could only be prototype states.

The tension

Less effort means more guessing.

Parents wanted listing to take seconds. But the less they type, the more the app has to guess — and a wrong guess costs trust.

The turning point

When research didn’t go to plan.

Parents didn’t want to be interviewed. So I changed how I listened.

I planned formal interviews. Early on, some parents told me they felt uncomfortable with them. Instead of pushing, I kept three short semi-structured interviews and added quick surveys — and went to where parents already were: my own circle, local parks and daycare.

What it taught me

Respect a parent’s time. If a formal interview felt like too much, a long listing form would too. That idea runs through every decision below.

03 — Key decisions

Three decisions, and why.

Decision 01

Start with a photo, not a form.

Why

Parents gave items away because listing took too long.

The alternative

A long form with autofill and saved defaults.

The trade-off

AI saves typing, but it can guess wrong.

What I chose

AI drafts. The parent checks and posts.

Decision 02

Make trust visible before anyone pays.

Why

Parents couldn’t judge a stranger’s item.

The alternative

Star ratings alone, like most marketplaces.

The trade-off

A score on its own hides the reason.

What I chose

Trust Score with verification and condition labels.

Decision 03

Search by the child, not the catalogue.

Why

Parents shop by age and size, not brand.

The alternative

A full filter panel, like adult fashion apps.

The trade-off

More filters would make search heavier.

What I chose

Three filters only: age, size, condition.

What I left out, on purpose

Payments, verification and photo analysis are prototype states, not real services. I spent my time on the flows parents said were painful, not on back-end work a team would build.

05

Outcome & reflection

The outcome

A 16-screen Figma prototype and a coded front-end prototype: listing starts from a photo, search starts from a child’s age and size, and seller trust is visible before anyone pays.

How I checked it

I ran a heuristic evaluation against Nielsen’s ten heuristics and logged nine issues. Three of them changed the design: the AI gave no sign it was working (#1 visibility of system status) — I added an “Analysing your photo…” state with a cancel; a failed upload left the parent stuck with nothing to do (#9 help users recover from errors) — I added an error screen with Retry upload and Save as draft; and a wrong AI guess could reach the listing unchecked (#5 error prevention) — I added a preview step before publishing. There is no user testing yet, so I can’t claim time saved or items sold.

What I’d test next

Watch parents list an item from one photo. Count how often they edit each AI suggestion, and where they hesitate before paying.

A pattern I’m working on

I start drawing before I’ve written down what I’m deciding. Now every feature gets a one-line “why”, tied to a finding, before I open Figma.

07

Writing the interface

I wrote every label, button and message in the app.

A parent holding a toddler reads about four words. So I kept one name for each idea, wrote buttons as actions, and made sure nothing silent ever happened on screen.

Say what is happening

“Analysing your photo…”

A spinner on its own looks like a crash. The words tell a parent the app is working and that the wait is theirs to expect.

Name the guess

“AI Confirmed” · “AI Suggested”

Two labels, used the same way on every listing, so a parent knows which details to check before posting.

An error needs a way out

“Something went wrong” → Retry upload · Save as draft

The message says what failed and offers two exits, so no one loses a photo session they can’t repeat.

Two words beat ten

“Buy now” · “Make an offer”

Every button says what happens next. Side by side, they make the choice obvious without a word of explanation.

One more rule I kept: a caution before a commitment, not after. “Double check your details before publishing” sits above the preview, where a parent can still change something.
